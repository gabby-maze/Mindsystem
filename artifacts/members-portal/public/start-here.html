The Real Problem
You're Not Confused About Volleyball.
You're Confused About Your Role In It.

Your daughter is 13 or 14. She's on a club team. She's getting reps. She's in the gym three, four, five days a week. You're spending real money to make sure she has every opportunity.

And something still isn't clicking.

Not her serve. Not her footwork. Something harder to name. The way she shuts down after a mistake. The car ride home that goes quiet or sideways. The way she can't seem to play the way she practices. The way she looks at you in the stands.

"What's happening in the gym is a symptom. What's happening in the family is the system."

The physical investment is there. The mental and intangible investment — the part that determines how she processes failure, receives feedback, and builds a competitive identity — that part hasn't been coached. And it won't happen by accident.

That's where Gabby works.

Why Nothing Else Is Working
You've Probably Already Tried
Some Version of This.

And it didn't stick. Not because you didn't care — because none of it addresses the actual gap.

More Private Lessons
Technical reps don't address the moments between the reps. The mindset, the reset, the identity — those don't get better with repetition alone.
More Tournaments
Competition exposes the gaps. It doesn't close them. Throwing her into more pressure without a framework for processing it just reinforces the patterns you're trying to break.
Therapy or Counseling
General mental health support isn't sport-specific. What athletes and their families need is performance language — a shared framework, not just a place to talk about feelings.
Talking to Her More
More conversation without structure adds noise, not clarity. Athletes don't need more advice from the stands. They need parents who know their role — and play it.
Why Gabby
She Didn't Learn This
in a Classroom.

Gabby Cole played professionally. She grew up inside the competitive volleyball system — not watching it, living it. She knows what the car ride home feels like from both seats.

After her playing career, she noticed the same pattern in every family she worked with: the athlete was being coached, but the family dynamic — the thing that either accelerates or sabotages everything else — was completely unaddressed.

So she built a system. Not a theory. A framework tested across real families, real seasons, real stakes. It's called the MindSystem, and it gives athletes and parents a shared language for the work that doesn't happen in the gym.

"The coaches are coaching the game. Gabby coaches the family."
